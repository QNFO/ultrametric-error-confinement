## 1. Purpose

The empirical test designs in 0.9.md (Tests A/B/C) focus on phylogenetics and synthetic data. This document proposes two additional domain-specific tests that operationalize the tree framework's falsifiable claim in **cognitive psychology** and **linguistics** — domains where hierarchical organization is hypothesized but not definitively established.

---

## 2. Experiment A: Human Similarity Judgment

### 2.1 Domain

Cognitive psychology: human perception of similarity between familiar categories.

### 2.2 Motivation

The tree framework claims that hierarchical domains produce ultrametric distance structures. Human categorical perception is widely believed to be hierarchically organized (e.g., animal taxonomy is nested: mammal → primate → human). If this is correct, **human similarity judgments for hierarchically organized categories should satisfy triadic rigidity significantly more often than similarity judgments for non-hierarchically organized stimuli.**

### 2.3 Hypothesis

**H1:** For hierarchically organized categories (e.g., animals), the Triadic Rigidity Index (TRI) computed from human similarity ratings will be significantly above chance level ($p < 0.01$, Cohen's $d > 1.0$).

**H2:** For non-hierarchically organized stimuli (e.g., color perception), TRI will NOT be significantly above chance ($p > 0.05$).

**H3:** TRI(animals) > TRI(colors) with statistical significance ($p < 0.01$).

### 2.4 Method

**Data sources (candidate datasets):**

| Dataset | Items | Structure | Reference |
|:--------|:------|:----------|:-----------|
| Animal similarity ratings | 30-50 common animals | Hypothesized hierarchical (genus → family → order → class) | Nosofsky (1991), or published norming studies |
| Color similarity ratings | 30-50 color chips | Hypothesized non-hierarchical (perceptual space is continuous) | Published color-naming studies (Berlin & Kay, 1969) |
| Artifact similarity | 30-50 common objects | Mixed — some hierarchy (furniture → chair → armchair), some flat | Published concept-norming datasets |

**Procedure:**
1. Obtain published human similarity-rating matrices (pairwise ratings of "how similar are A and B?" on a 1–7 scale, or derived from confusion probabilities).
2. Convert similarity to distance: $d(x, y) = 1 - \text{sim}(x, y) / \max(\text{sim})$ or $d(x, y) = \max(\text{sim}) - \text{sim}(x, y)$.
3. Compute TRI for all $\binom{n}{3}$ triples using the tolerance method from 0.9.py.
4. Permutation test (1000 shuffles) to compare observed TRI to null distribution.
5. Report $p$-value and Cohen's $d$ for each domain.

**Tolerance parameter:** Use the scale-invariant tolerance from 0.9.py: $\epsilon = 0.01 \cdot \bar{d}$ where $\bar{d}$ is the mean pairwise distance.

### 2.5 Success Criteria

| Criterion | Threshold | Interpretation if met |
|:----------|:----------|:----------------------|
| TRI(animals) > chance | $p < 0.01$ | Human similarity perception for animals is tree-structured |
| TRI(colors) NOT > chance | $p > 0.05$ | Color perception is NOT tree-structured (as predicted) |
| TRI(animals) > TRI(colors) | $p < 0.01$, Cohen's $d > 1.0$ | The framework correctly discriminates hierarchical from non-hierarchical domains in human cognition |

### 2.6 Failure Modes

| Outcome | Interpretation |
|:--------|:---------------|
| TRI(animals) = chance level | Human similarity perception for animals is NOT tree-structured — the framework's cognitive extension is falsified |
| TRI(colors) >> chance | Color perception IS tree-structured — the framework's domain boundary is wrong, or the distance measure is inappropriate |
| Both above chance | All human similarity perception is tree-structured — the framework's discriminatory claim is false (no non-hierarchical domain exists in cognition) |
| Both at chance | Measurement noise dominates — the methodology needs refinement (larger $n$, better similarity measures) |

## 3. Experiment B: Linguistic Lexicostatistics

### 3.1 Domain

Historical linguistics: lexicostatistical distances between languages in the same family.

### 3.2 Motivation

Language families are the paradigmatic example of tree-structured historical relationships. The comparative method in linguistics explicitly reconstructs branching phylogenies. If the tree framework is correct, **lexicostatistical distances within a well-established language family should be approximately ultrametric**, with high TRI values.

### 3.3 Hypothesis

**H4:** For a well-established language family (e.g., Indo-European), TRI computed from lexicostatistical distances will be significantly above chance ($p < 0.001$, Cohen's $d > 2.0$), approaching 1.0.

**H5:** If borrowings and areal effects are controlled for (using only basic vocabulary), TRI will be significantly higher than when all vocabulary is included (because borrowings introduce non-tree-like distances).

### 3.4 Method

**Data sources:**

| Dataset | Languages | Items | Reference |
|:--------|:----------|:------|:-----------|
| IELex (Indo-European Lexical Database) | 50+ IE languages | 200+ Swadesh-list concepts | Dunn et al. (2011), or IELex public release |
| ASJP (Automated Similarity Judgment Program) | 100+ languages worldwide | 40-item list | Holman et al. (2008) |
| Glottolog | Global language classification | Reference trees | Hammarstrom et al. (annual) |

**Procedure:**
1. Select 20-30 languages from a single well-established family (e.g., Indo-European: English, German, Dutch, Swedish, French, Spanish, Italian, Russian, Polish, Hindi, Persian, etc.).
2. Obtain cognate judgments for a standard word list (Swadesh 100 or 200 items). For each word, code whether each language pair shares a cognate (1) or not (0).
3. Compute pairwise lexicostatistical distance: fraction of word-list items where languages lack a shared cognate. $d(i,j) = 1 - \frac{\text{shared cognates}}{\text{total items compared}}$.
4. Compute TRI for all language triples.
5. Compare to null distribution from permutation test.
6. Optional: compare TRI for basic vocabulary (Swadesh 100, resistant to borrowing) vs. cultural vocabulary (more borrowing-prone). Prediction: TRI(basic) > TRI(cultural).

### 3.5 Success Criteria

| Criterion | Threshold | Interpretation |
|:----------|:----------|:---------------|
| TRI(IE basic vocab) | $\geq 0.90$ | Lexicostatistical distances are near-perfectly ultrametric |
| TRI(IE) > chance | $p < 0.001$ | Language family distances are tree-structured |
| TRI(basic) > TRI(cultural) | $p < 0.05$ | Borrowings degrade tree structure — framework correctly identifies noise |

### 3.6 Failure Modes

| Outcome | Interpretation |
|:--------|:---------------|
| TRI(IE) at chance level | Lexicostatistical distances are NOT tree-structured — either the data is too noisy, the cognate judgments are unreliable, or language relationships are not purely tree-like (network model may be more appropriate) |
| TRI(IE) high but not > TRI(cultural) | Borrowings do not degrade tree structure — borrowings may be too rare in the word list to matter, or the tree signal dominates |
