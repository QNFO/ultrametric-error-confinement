## 1. Formal Definitions

### 1.1 Rooted Tree

A **rooted tree** is an ordered pair $T = (V, E)$ where:

- $V$ is a finite set of **nodes**
- $E \subseteq V \times V$ is a set of directed edges (parent $\to$ child)
- There exists a unique node $r \in V$ called the **root** with no incoming edges
- Every other node $v \in V \setminus \{r\}$ has exactly one parent
- $T$ is connected: there is a unique directed path from $r$ to every $v \in V$

The set of **leaves** is $L(T) = \{v \in V : v \text{ has no outgoing edges}\}$.
The set of **internal nodes** is $I(T) = V \setminus L(T)$.

For $x, y \in L(T)$, the **lowest common ancestor** (LCA), denoted $\operatorname{lca}(x, y)$, is the unique internal node that is an ancestor of both $x$ and $y$ and has no descendant that is also an ancestor of both.

### 1.2 Height Function

A **height function** is a mapping $h: V \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ satisfying:

1. $h(v) = 0$ for all leaves $v \in L(T)$
2. $h(u) < h(v)$ whenever $u$ is a child of $v$ (heights strictly increase toward the root)

The height of an internal node encodes the dissimilarity threshold at which the clusters below it merge. Larger heights mean coarser distinctions (closer to root); smaller heights mean finer distinctions (closer to leaves).

**Interpretation:**
- $h(\operatorname{lca}(x, y))$ answers: "How far back toward the trunk must I travel before $x$ and $y$ part company?"
- The height measures *how soon* the items diverge — a large LCA height means they diverge early (at a coarse cut); a small LCA height means they diverge late (only after many fine cuts).

### 1.3 Cophenetic Distance

The **cophenetic distance** between two leaves $x, y \in L(T)$ is:

$$d(x, y) = h(\operatorname{lca}(x, y))$$

This is the standard definition used in hierarchical clustering (Sokal & Rohlf, 1962; Sneath & Sokal, 1973). The distance between $x$ and $y$ is the dissimilarity value at which they are first assigned to the same cluster.

**Key properties (informal):**

| Property | Meaning |
|:---------|:--------|
| $d(x, x) = 0$ | An item is indistinguishable from itself (LCA of $x$ with $x$ is $x$, height 0) |
| $d(x, y) = d(y, x)$ | Symmetry: distance from $x$ to $y$ equals distance from $y$ to $x$ |
| $d(x, y) > 0$ for $x \neq y$ | Distinct items always have positive distance |
| $d(x, z) \leq \max(d(x, y), d(y, z))$ | Ultrametric inequality (see §3) |

### 1.4 Information-Theoretic Interpretation

In a binary decision tree where each cut represents a yes/no question:

- The shared path length (number of questions $x$ and $y$ answer identically before diverging) is proportional to $-\log_2(d(x, y))$ when heights are chosen appropriately
- **"Distance equals bits"**: For constant branch-length increments, cophenetic distance equals the number of questions that separate two items — i.e., the minimum description length of their difference

## 2. Worked Example

### 2.1 A Four-Item Dendrogram

Consider four items distinguished by two binary attributes:

| Item | Color | Size |
|:-----|:------|:-----|
| A | Red | Large |
| B | Red | Small |
| C | Blue | Large |
| D | Blue | Small |

The natural hierarchical clustering first splits by color (coarse distinction), then by size (fine distinction):

```
              Height
               ↑
               |
      h = 5   *  (root: all items together)
             / \
            /   \
      h=3  *     *  (color cut: red-left, blue-right)
          / \   / \
         /   \ /   \
    h=0 A   B C     D  (size cut: large-left, small-right)
```

### 2.2 Computing Pairwise Distances

| Pair | LCA | $h(\operatorname{lca})$ | $d(x, y)$ | Interpretation |
|:-----|:----|:------------------------|:----------|:---------------|
| A–B | color=red node | 3 | 3 | Close — share color, differ only in size |
| C–D | color=blue node | 3 | 3 | Close — share color, differ only in size |
| A–C | root | 5 | 5 | Far — diverge at very first (color) cut |
| A–D | root | 5 | 5 | Far — diverge at very first cut |
| B–C | root | 5 | 5 | Far — diverge at very first cut |
| B–D | root | 5 | 5 | Far — diverge at very first cut |

**Cophenetic distance matrix:**

$$
D = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & 3 & 5 & 5 \\
3 & 0 & 5 & 5 \\
5 & 5 & 0 & 3 \\
5 & 5 & 3 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\quad
\begin{array}{c}
\text{A} \\
\text{B} \\
\text{C} \\
\text{D}
\end{array}
$$

**Interpretation:** Items in the same color (A–B, C–D) are closer ($d = 3$) than items across colors ($d = 5$). The distance encodes the depth of the cut that separates them: color is a deeper (earlier) cut than size, so crossing color lines costs more distance.

### 2.3 Interpreting Cophenetic Distance Intuitively

The original description captures this exactly:

> "Two items are close when they stay together through many fine cuts before a line finally separates them. They are far when the very first cut that divides them is a broad, shallow one."

- A and B stay together through the color cut (they're both red), then through the size cut... and only at the very end does a line separate "large red" from "small red." They share 2 of the 2 cuts. **Distance = 3 (small).**
- A and C separate at the very first cut — "red anything" vs. "blue anything." They share 0 cuts beyond the root. **Distance = 5 (large).**

## 3. The Ultrametric Inequality

### 3.1 Statement

For any three leaves $x, y, z \in L(T)$:

$$d(x, z) \leq \max\!\big(d(x, y),\; d(y, z)\big)$$

This is **stronger** than the ordinary triangle inequality $d(x, z) \leq d(x, y) + d(y, z)$. The ultrametric inequality says the longest side is at most the max of the other two — which forces all triangles to be **acute isosceles** (two equal sides, both at least as long as the third).

### 3.2 Proof from Tree Structure

**Step 1: The LCA property.** For any three leaves $x, y, z$ in a rooted tree, among their three pairwise lowest common ancestors, at least two are identical. That is, among $\{\operatorname{lca}(x, y), \operatorname{lca}(y, z), \operatorname{lca}(x, z)\}$, two are the same node.

*Proof of LCA property:* Consider the three paths from root to each leaf. Let $N = \operatorname{lca}(x, y, z)$ — the LCA of all three. The three paths to $x, y, z$ branch from $N$. Since $N$ can have only finitely many children, at least two of $\{x, y, z\}$ must descend through the same child of $N$. Suppose $x$ and $y$ descend through the same child of $N$. Then $\operatorname{lca}(x, y)$ is strictly deeper than $N$, while $\operatorname{lca}(x, z) = \operatorname{lca}(y, z) = N$. Thus two LCAs coincide. $\square$

**Step 2: Height ordering.** Let the two coincident LCAs be at node $M$ (height $h(M)$), and let the third LCA be at node $N$ (height $h(N)$). Because $M$ and $N$ lie on the same root-to-leaf path (both are ancestors of some leaf), and $M$ is either equal to $N$ or a descendant of $N$:

$$h(M) \leq h(N)$$

with equality iff $M = N$.

**Step 3: Apply to distances.** Two of the three distances equal $h(M)$, and the third equals $h(N)$. Without loss of generality, suppose:

$$d(x, y) = h(M), \quad d(y, z) = h(N), \quad d(x, z) = h(N)$$

Then:

$$d(x, z) = h(N) \leq \max(h(M), h(N)) = \max(d(x, y), d(y, z))$$

which is exactly the ultrametric inequality. If all three LCAs coincide ($M = N$), then all three distances equal $h(N)$, and the inequality holds with equality. $\square$

### 3.3 Verification on the Worked Example

For triple $\{\text{A}, \text{B}, \text{C}\}$:

- $d(\text{A}, \text{B}) = 3$ (LCA at color=red node, $h = 3$)
- $d(\text{B}, \text{C}) = 5$ (LCA at root, $h = 5$)
- $d(\text{A}, \text{C}) = 5$ (LCA at root, $h = 5$)

Check: $d(\text{A}, \text{C}) = 5 \leq \max(3, 5) = 5$. ✓

For triple $\{\text{A}, \text{C}, \text{D}\}$:

- $d(\text{A}, \text{C}) = 5$, $d(\text{C}, \text{D}) = 3$, $d(\text{A}, \text{D}) = 5$
- $5 \leq \max(5, 3) = 5$ ✓

All 4 choose-3 = 4 triples satisfy the inequality.

## 4. Triadic Rigidity

### 4.1 Statement

For any three leaves $x, y, z$ in a cophenetic distance space, the **two largest** of $\{d(x, y), d(y, z), d(x, z)\}$ are equal.

### 4.2 Derivation

**Proof:** By the LCA property (§3.2, Step 1), two of the three pairwise LCAs coincide. Let these be at height $h(M)$, and the third at height $h(N)$, with $h(M) \leq h(N)$.

The three distances are $\{h(M), h(N), h(N)\}$. The two largest are $\{h(N), h(N)\}$, which are trivially equal. If $h(M) = h(N)$ (all three LCAs coincide), then all three distances equal $h(N)$ and the two largest are equal. $\square$

**Consequence:** In any ultrametric space, every triangle is an **acute isosceles** triangle. Equivalently: there are no scalene triangles, no obtuse triangles. The triangular "shape" of any three items is rigidly constrained — two sides must match.

### 4.3 Triadic Rigidity as Ontic Signature

This rigidity is not an arbitrary mathematical curiosity. It is the **structural signature of hierarchical organization**:

| If distances are... | Then the underlying structure is... |
|:--------------------|:------------------------------------|
| Ultrametric (triadic rigidity holds) | Hierarchical — items organized in nested clusters |
| Metric but not ultrametric (triadic rigidity fails) | Non-hierarchical — items related by pairwise proximities without global nesting |

Triadic rigidity thus provides a **falsifiable empirical claim**: if any triple of items in a domain violates triadic rigidity, that domain is not purely hierarchically organized.

### 4.4 Example of Violation (Non-Hierarchical Distance)

Consider three cities with Euclidean distances:
- d(NYC, Philly) = 150 km
- d(NYC, Boston) = 350 km
- d(Philly, Boston) = 500 km

The two largest are $\{500, 350\}$, which are unequal. Triadic rigidity fails. This domain (geographic distance) is not purely hierarchical — it is spatial, where distances are additive, not nested.

## 5. Binary Decision Trees

### 5.1 Universal Representational Capacity

Any rooted tree with multi-way branching can be decomposed into a binary tree by introducing artificial internal nodes. Every $k$-way split at a node is equivalent to $k-1$ binary splits.

**Theorem:** For any finite set $\mathcal{X}$ of items and any nested partition hierarchy on $\mathcal{X}$, there exists a binary rooted tree $T$ whose leaves are exactly $\mathcal{X}$ and whose branching structure reproduces the partition hierarchy.

**Proof sketch:** For each internal node with $m$ children, create $m-1$ new internal nodes forming a chain of binary splits. Each binary split separates one child cluster from the rest. Heights are assigned to preserve the ultrametric inequality. $\square$

**Implication:** Binary distinctions (yes/no questions) are the **atomic operation** of hierarchical organization. All complexity reduces to their iteration.

### 5.2 Information-Theoretic Gauge

If we assign a constant height increment $\Delta$ per binary cut, then:

$$d(x, y) = \Delta \cdot (\text{number of binary cuts separating } x \text{ from } y)$$

With $\Delta = 1$ (measuring in "bits"):

$$d(x, y) = \text{shared path length before separation in bits}$$

**Example:** In the worked example (§2), with $\Delta = 1$ and root height = 2 cuts:

| Pair | Shared cuts | Distance (bits) |
|:-----|:------------|:----------------|
| A–B | 1 (color only) | 1 |
| A–C | 0 | 2 |

**"Distance equals bits"** — similarity between two items is measured by how many binary questions they answer identically before separating.

### 5.3 Gauge Freedom

The choice of $\Delta$ (constant increment) is a **gauge choice**. Any monotone-increasing assignment of heights that preserves the branching order is equally valid:

$$h'(v) = f(h(v))$$

for any strictly increasing function $f$. The branching order (which items share which cuts) is the **invariant**; the numerical distances are the **gauge**.

This is precisely analogous to the Page–Wootters construction in quantum gravity: the Wheeler–DeWitt equation defines a timeless structure (the tree); the Page–Wootters internal clock (the cophenetic distance) extracts a time parameter by selecting a degree of freedom as a "clock" (the height function). Different choices of clock (different height assignments) produce different numerical time readings, but all preserve the same causal/topological order.

## 6. Formal Summary

### 6.1 The Threefold Structure

| Layer | Mathematical Object | Ontological Role | Epistemological Role |
|:------|:--------------------|:-----------------|:---------------------|
| **Topology** | Rooted tree $(V, E)$ with LCA structure | The timeless, ontic branching order | Which items are hierarchically related and how |
| **Gauge** | Height function $h: V \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ | The choice of numerical scale | How "far apart" items are in a chosen metric |
| **Metric** | Cophenetic distance $d(x, y) = h(\operatorname{lca}(x, y))$ | The emergent pairwise comparison | Operational dissimilarity, used for ranking and thresholding |

### 6.2 Key Theorems

| Theorem | Statement | Significance |
|:--------|:----------|:-------------|
| **Ultrametric inequality** | $d(x, z) \leq \max(d(x, y), d(y, z))$ | Stronger than triangle inequality; signature of tree structure |
| **Triadic rigidity** | Two largest distances among any triple are equal | Empirical fingerprint of hierarchical organization |
| **Binary decomposition** | Every multi-way tree decomposes to binary | Yes/no is the atomic operation of distinction |
| **Gauge invariance** | $d'(x, y) = f(d(x, y))$ for strictly increasing $f$ preserves ultrametric property | The branching order is invariant; specific distances are conventional |

### 6.3 The Core Insight

> **"Distinction is primary; measurement is secondary."**

The tree — the structure of who separates from whom, at what level of nesting — is the **ontic** reality. The cophenetic distance — the numerical value assigned to each separation — is the **epistemic** construction. Different observers may assign different heights (different gauges), producing different numerical matrices, but all agree on the branching order (the invariant).
